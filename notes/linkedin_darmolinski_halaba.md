Russia Is Developing Geran Drones Along Three Main Paths

Russia no longer treats the Geran solely as a cheap long-range one-way attack drone flying toward pre-programmed coordinates. The modifications observed today indicate that the entire family is being developed in three parallel directions.

The first is higher speed. Alongside the classic piston-powered Geran-2, jet-powered variants are appearing. They reduce the defender’s reaction time and are more difficult to intercept with mobile fire groups and some of the interceptor drones currently in service.

The second direction is communications, remote control and electro-optical guidance. Additional antennas are visible on newer airframes, while earlier examined examples contained cameras, radio modems and onboard computers designed for image processing. This configuration can allow the operator to receive live video, correct the flight path after launch and select the target only after the drone reaches the attack area. In practice, this means that the drone may engage not only pre-designated fixed targets, but also vehicles, boats, railway transport and other moving objects.

The third direction is the development of specialised variants. Reconnaissance, relay and air-defence-hunting versions are emerging, along with experimental configurations carrying additional weapons. A further, previously undocumented Geran-2 variant has now appeared, fitted with extra antennas mounted on the wings. Their exact purpose still requires confirmation, but they are most likely connected with enhanced communications, data transmission or remote-control capabilities.

The Geran is therefore gradually ceasing to be merely a “flying bomb” and is becoming a family of platforms adapted to different missions. The greatest threat comes from combining remote control, optical observation and automatic target tracking. In such a configuration, the drone can fly most of its route autonomously, then receive corrections from an operator in the terminal phase or continue the attack independently after locking onto the selected target.

The Ukrainian side has also published a map showing the potential operating range of remotely controlled Geran drones. Areas marked in red are described as threat zones in which such drones may attack both fixed and moving targets. The main potential targets include warehouses, fuel stations, industrial plants, energy infrastructure, logistics facilities, vehicles, boats and railway transport.

The red zone should not, however, be treated as a fixed and absolute technical boundary.The actual range depends on the type of datalink, flight altitude, terrain, the location of relay stations and electronic-warfare conditions. It should primarily be understood as an area of increased risk, where owners and employees of critical facilities should take the possibility of a targeted strike into account, respond to alerts and prepare protected shelters.

### Post 2 • 3 days ago • post własny

Orion with the S8000 Banderol Missile

Russia has unveiled the Orion/Inokhodets UAV carrying the S8000 Banderol cruise missile. This is not merely a new weapon for a drone. It is an attempt to create a relatively inexpensive, reusable stand-off strike carrier.

The Orion does notneed to penetrate deep into enemy-controlled airspace. It can remain over Russian territory, occupied areas or the Black Sea and launch the missile from a safer distance. The key advantage is the mobility of the launch point. Unlike a ground-based launcher, the UAV can change its position and attack direction, making the incoming threat harder to predict.

According to available data, the S8000 has a range of around 500 kilometres and a speed of approximately 520 to 650 km/h. It is neither particularly fast nor technologically revolutionary. Its significance may come from lower cost, simpler production and the possibility of fielding it in larger numbers.

A single Orion is not the main threat. The real problem begins when the Banderol is integrated into a mixed strike package together with Geran drones, decoys, cruise missiles and ballistic missiles. Another type of target, a different flight profile and an additional attack vector all increase the burden on air defence.

Available images suggest that the Orion is used here mainly as a carrier. Its standard electro-optical turret may have been reduced or removed to free up weight and space under the fuselage. Targeting data can be supplied by other reconnaissance platforms, electronic intelligence systems or satellite sources.

There is, however, no basis for claiming that the Orion has an anti-submarine warfare capability. It may be useful over the sea for reconnaissance and for engaging surface targets, but there is no confirmed integration of sonobuoys, a magnetic anomaly detector or torpedoes.

The Orion with the Banderol is not a wonder weapon. It is, however, another element of Russia’s air attack architecture, where the decisive factors are not the perfection of a single weapon, but scale, diversity and the ability to overload air defence.

**▶ POLEMIKA Z HALABĄ: krytyka podręcznika "Operator i Konstruktor BSP"**

Na rynku pojawił się kolejny „podręcznik” dla operatorów i konstruktorów BSP. I dobrze, że temat dronów przebija się szerzej, bo edukacji w Polsce naprawdę brakuje.

Problem zaczyna się wtedy, gdy materiał próbuje budować narrację ekspercką, a pod spodem widać brak pełnego zrozumienia fizyki lotu, aerodynamiki, pracy kontrolera lotu i praktycznej konfiguracji platformy.

Autor najwyraźniej widział trochę materiałów, pewnie także z kierunku ukraińskiego, zna część haseł, wie, jak brzmi język środowiska i próbuje wejść w rolę „specjalisty od wszystkiego”. Tylko że w BSP sama znajomość słów nie wystarcza. Tu bardzo szybko wychodzi, czy ktoś naprawdę rozumie, co dzieje się z platformą w powietrzu.

W analizowanym materiale problematyczne są szczególnie trzy obszary:

Fizyka lotu multirotora

Pojawiają się uproszczenia i niespójności w opisie relacji ciągu, ciężaru i pochylenia platformy. To są podstawy.Jeżeli kursant źle zrozumie ten mechanizm, będzie źle rozumiał każdy kolejny etap szkolenia.

Aerodynamika śmigła i opór

Temat jest opisany zbyt szkolnie i zbyt płasko. Przy podręczniku dla „operatora i konstruktora BSP” nie wystarczy powiedzieć, że śmigło działa, bo powietrze przepływa szybciej z jednej strony. To buduje złe intuicje.

Ustawienia sterowania, FC, rates i PID

Część dotycząca konfiguracji jest zbyt uproszczona. Pomija praktyczne elementy, które decydują o zachowaniu platformy: expo, center sensitivity, deadband, throttle curve, endpoints, filtrację, logi, objawy złego PID i procedurę testowania zmian.

To nie jest kwestia czepiania się przecinków. Mówimy o materiale, który ma szkolić ludzi w obszarze, gdzie błędna wiedza przekłada się na złe nawyki, rozbite platformy i niebezpieczne decyzje w powietrzu.

Dlatego w najbliższym czasie na łamach Fundacji Żelazny opublikujemy cykl krótkich artykułów, w których spokojnie itechnicznie pokażemy błędy w tym podręczniku. Bez personalnych wycieczek. Punkt po punkcie: fizyka, aerodynamika, konfiguracja, prawo, metodyka szkolenia.

Bo jeżeli ktoś chce uczyć operatorów i konstruktorów BSP, musi najpierw sam rozumieć podstawy. A tutaj, niestety, widać więcej autopromocji niż solidnej wiedzy.

### Post 7 • 2 weeks ago • post własny

Jesteśmy w punkcie zwrotnym walki o niebo.

Ostatnie ataki pokazują zmianę sposobu użycia rodziny Geran/Shahed. To nie jest już tylko masowe wypuszczanie wolnych dronów tłokowych, które mają przeciążyć obronę i częścią salwy przebić się do celu. Rosja zaczyna budować bardziej złożony pakiet uderzeniowy: wolne Gerany, cele pozorne, wersje reaktywne, łączność mesh, warianty z kamerami i funkcją przechwytywania celu, a do tego rakiety manewrujące oraz balistyczne.

W nocy z 5 na 6 lipca Rosja użyła przeciw Ukrainie 419 środków napadu powietrznego: 351 dronów i 68 rakiet. Ukraina zneutralizowała 363 cele, w tym 326 dronów i 37 rakiet manewrujących. Kluczowe jest jednak to, że rakiety balistyczne i Cyrkony nie zostały przechwycone. Ten atak pokazuje więc nie tylko skalę salwy, ale też sposób jej budowania: najpierw masa dronów i wabików, potem rakiety manewrujące, a w końcowej fazie środki o najkrótszym czasie reakcji dla obrony.

Równolegle zmienia się sam Geran. Klasyczny Geran-2/Shahed-136 to wolna platforma tłokowa, lecąca zwykle około 150–190 km/h, z głowicą rzędu 40–50 kg. Taki dron jest groźny dzięki skali i cenie, ale daje obronie czas. Przy 180 km/h pokonanie 50 km zajmuje mu około 17 minut. To czas na wykrycie, klasyfikację, naprowadzenie mobilnej grupy ogniowej albo użycie tańszego efektora.

Wersje reaktywne zmieniają tę kalkulację. Według danych HUR Geran-4 osiąga do 500 km/h, ma pułap do 5 km, zasięg do 450 km i może przenosić głowicę 50 kg albo powiększoną głowicę termobaryczną 90 kg. Dron lecący 500 km/h pokonuje 50 km w około 6 minut. To już nie jest profil klasycznego wolnego Shaheda. To zaczyna przypominać tani pocisk manewrujący produkowany w logice dronowej, czyli szybciej, taniej i masowo.

Najważniejsza zmiana dotyczy jednak nie tylko napędu, ale też łączności i sposobu naprowadzania. W ujawnionych danych dla Geran-4 wskazano obecność modemu mesh Xingkay Tech. W wariantach typu Geran Seeker opisywane są dodatkowe kamery, modemy mesh oraz funkcja automatycznego uchwycenia celu. To oznacza odejście od prostego schematu: zaprogramowana trasa, GNSS, uderzenie w koordynaty. Nowy schemat to lot po trasie, możliwość korekty, transmisja obrazu, praca operatora lub algorytmu i naprowadzanie terminalne na wykryty cel.

Widać też rozdzielenie zadań między różne wersje Geranów. W głębi kraju, gdzie Ukraina coraz skuteczniej zwalcza wolne drony, rośnie znaczenie wersji reaktywnych. Przy granicy i na terenach przyfrontowych klasyczne benzynowe Gerany nadal są używane masowo, ale głównie przeciw celom infrastrukturalnym: stacjom paliw, magazynom, energetyce i transportowi. Według Serhija „Flasha” Beskrestnowa około 200 takich dronów dziennie atakuje cele w rejonach przygranicznych. Bliskość granicy pozwala tam szerzej wykorzystywać sterowanie radiowe przez modemy mesh, dlatego samo zakłócanie GNSS ma ograniczoną skuteczność.

### Post 8 • 2 weeks ago • post własny

A Shift in Russian Tactics: FAB/KAB Bombs and the ZALA Z-16 as a System for Hunting the Frontline Rear Area

Information from the Ukrainian side points to a change in Russia’s use of guided aerial bombs. Only a few months ago, the enemy was testing strikes at significant depth, reportedly up to around 250 km inside Ukrainian territory. Now, Russia is increasingly using FAB/KAB bombs against settlements, crossings, buildings and facilities located along the front line.

This does not mean that Russia has abandoned longer-range employment. It is using that range differently, as a safety buffer for its aircraft. The aircraft can release the bomb farther from the line of contact, without entering the highest-risk zone ofUkrainian air defence. According to the Ukrainian account, the bombs are also being released along less predictable trajectories to make it harder to forecast the impact point and intercept them.

The second part of this picture is the ZALA Z-16. This is not just a camera used to record a strike. In larger variants, such as the Z-16-5G/T-16-5G, it is a reconnaissance platform with a take-off weight of up to around 49.5 kg, a wingspan of 4.64 m, a hybrid propulsion system, endurance of more than 12 hours and a declared video transmission range of over 150 km. This allows it to maintain long-duration observation over an area of operations without requiring the operator to work close to the front line.

Technically, ZALA performs an ISR/BDA role, meaning intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance and battle damage assessment. Its electro-optical payload allows it to monitor crossings, access roads, built-up areas, vehicle movement and concentration points. Inertial navigation, satellite correction and alternative navigation modes improve the system’s resilience in a jamming environment. Pneumatic launcher take-off and parachute recovery allow the system to be operated from prepared but relatively mobile positions.

In this model, ZALA detects the target, keeps it under observation, confirms activity, transmits imagery to the command post and then assesses the effect after the strike. This creates a full loop: reconnaissance, decision, strike and damage assessment.

FAB/KAB provides the heavy warhead and stand-off strike capability. ZALA provides target freshness and confirmation that the strike made sense. In this setup, a crossing, logistics route, building or concentration point is not attacked randomly. It is first observed, then struck, and then assessed to determine whether another strike is needed.

This changes how we should look at the ZALA Z-16. It is no longerjust a reconnaissance drone. It is a sensor plugged into a strike system. The greatest threat is not a single bomb or a single UAV, but their integration into an effective loop for hunting the frontline rear area.

### Post 9 • 4 months ago • repost od: Fundacja Żelazny

Wojna znowu zmieniła swoje zasady.

Na wielu odcinkach frontu nie rozstrzyga już sama piechota ani nawet klasycznie rozumiany pancerz. O przewadze decyduje dziś to, kto szybciej wykryje ruch, kto dłużej utrzyma obserwację i kto wcześniej naprowadzi uderzenie. Drony przejęły ogromną część pola walki.

To nie jest pierwszy taki moment w historii. W czasie I wojny światowej podobny przełom przyniosły karabin maszynowy i nowoczesna artyleria. Nagle okazało się, że dawny model natarcia przestał działać. Sama odwaga piechoty nie wystarczała, a wojna ugrzęzła w okopach.

Później pojawił się czołg, początkowo zawodny, ale przełomowy, bo przywracał ruch tam, gdzie człowiek sam nie miał już większych szans. W II wojnie światowej poziom trudności wzrósł jeszcze bardziej. O wyniku starć decydowały już całe systemy działania: manewr, logistyka, łączność, lotnictwo i przemysł.

Dziś jesteśmy w podobnym punkcie zwrotnym.

Drony nie są już dodatkiem do wojny. Zmieniają jej logikę. Ograniczają ruch, wymuszają rozproszenie, utrudniają koncentrację sił i sprawiają, że każda kolumna, pojazd czy pojedynczy żołnierz może zostać wykryty i zaatakowany dużo szybciej niż kiedyś.

Dlatego samo dosyłanie kolejnych ludzi nie rozwiąże problemu. Tak jak sto lat temu nie dało się przełamać frontu samą masą piechoty pod ogniem karabinów maszynowych, tak dziś nie da się odzyskać manewru bez masowego systemu ochrony przed dronami.

Potrzebny jest nowy etap technologiczny. Nie tylko więcej stali, ale więcej zdolności: wykrywanie, zakłócanie, aktywna osłona i niszczenie sensorów przeciwnika oraz tanich środków napadu z powietrza.

Na obecnym etapie przewagę ma ten, kto lata częściej, widzi więcej i uderza celniej. Ale historia pokazuje też coś jeszcze. Każda dominująca technologia w końcu doczekuje się odpowiedzi.

Pytanie nie brzmi już, czy wojna sięzmieniła. To już się stało. Pytanie brzmi, kto pierwszy zbuduje system, który znowu pozwoli przetrwać, poruszać się i przełamywać front.

What Does Iran Actually Have?

Not “just another drone,” but its own class of anti-air effectors

When discussing Iran’s unmanned capabilities, most attention goes to the Shahed family, while systems such as 358 and 359 receive far less attention. That is a mistake, because these systems represent one of the more interesting attempts to build a low-cost, asymmetric layer of air defense against aerial targets, especially UAVs.

358 is not a classic SAM in the Western sense.

It combines characteristics of a surface-to-air missile and a loitering munition. After launch, it can enter the engagement area and remain airborne while waiting for a target. In practical terms, this shifts the model from “detect, launch,intercept” toward inserting the effector into the battlespace and keeping it available for interception. Western sources have described it as a loitering SAM.

This is not just terminology. It reflects a different employment architecture.

In conventional air defense, the cost of interception rises quickly, especially against cheap reconnaissance or strike drones. Iran appears to have pursued an intermediate solution: an effector able to spend longer periods in the battlespace, with a relatively small logistical footprint, and useful against slower aerial targets. DIA materials on intercepted arms shipments to Yemen indicate that 358 was not just a demonstrator, but a system that entered operational circulation.

A second important point is deployment simplicity. Public analyses of earlier seizures and presentations suggested that 358 can be launched from simple positions, without the heavy infrastructure usually associated with conventional SAM systems. That matters in irregular and distributed conflicts, where concealment, mobility, and rapid field deployment matter as much as raw performance.

Even more interesting is 359, presented since 2025 as a further development of the same concept. Available material suggests Iran is working on a version with greater altitude potential and higher speed, expanding this family from a mainly counter-UAV role toward more demanding aerial targets. Still, caution is necessary: much of the published data on 359 still comes from presentations and secondary reporting rather than broadly confirmed combat evidence.

The key point is not the numbers themselves.

The key point is the operational model Iran has been developing:

an effector cheaper than a conventional interceptor missile

the ability to remain airborne while waiting for a target

intended use against UAVs and other relatively slower aerial platforms

a low logistical threshold for deployment

the possibility of building a distributed, asymmetric air defense layer

That is why 358 and 359 should not be treated as “Iranian curiosities,” but as an indicator of where low-cost air defense is heading.

Iran does not only have strike drones.

It also has its own concept of an intercepting effector for the age of mass UAV warfare.

New Video Transmission Frequencies on the Ukrainian Front

This text opens a new series called “Echo of War”, where I look at how battlefield experience from Ukraine is reshaping drone technologies and electronic warfare. Unlike the series “Through the Enemy’s Eyes”, which focuses on analyzing specific adversary actions, this one looks at broader technological trends that are beginning to influence UAV development worldwide.

On the Ukrainian front it is becoming clear that the competition in drone warfare is also shifting into the video transmission spectrum. It is no longer only about transmitter power or antenna design. Increasingly, it is about choosing frequency ranges that are less saturated with the opponent’s jamming systems.

Several interesting hardware solutions have appeared in recent months.

One example is a new generation of transmitters from PeakFPV, operating in the 8–8.8 GHz band with power up to 4 W. Another example is a transmitter from ScreamIndustries, working in the 10–12 GHz range. There are also reports of systems installed on “Pegasus” drones operating across a very wide 7.5–12 GHz band.

In practical terms this means one thing: the spectrum used by FPV systems is rapidly expanding. Video transmission systems for drones can now operate anywhere from roughly 320 MHz up to 12 GHz.

The key reason is straightforward. Most field jammers were designed to operate against the most common bands: 433 MHz, 868/915 MHz, 2.4 GHz and 5.8 GHz. Frequencies above 8 GHz are far less covered by improvised electronic warfare systems deployed on the battlefield.

As a result, alongside the shift in frequency, we are also seeing modifications to the classic analog video standard and the growing use of frequency transverters.

Drone warfare is gradually becoming less about cheap flying platforms and more about engineering the radio architecture and controlling the electromagnetic spectrum.

On 27 January 2026, a civilian passenger train in motion in northeastern Ukraine was struck by Russian strike drones. At least one Shahed/Geran-type UAV hit a passenger carriage, not the locomotive or railway infrastructure. The train was carrying civilians, including women and children. The result was fatalities and injuries.

From a technical perspective, this attack illustrates how Russian strike drones have evolved from simple, autonomous loitering munitions into operator-controlled systems capable of deliberate target selection.

The Russian Geran-2, derived from the Iranian Shahed-136, was originally designed as a low-cost, “fire-and-forget” weapon. Early variants relied on pre-programmed GNSS/INS navigation and flew toward fixed coordinates without human involvement after launch. Their effectiveness came from mass use, not precision.

That model is no longer sufficient. Evidence collected between 2024 and 2026 indicates that newer Geran variants incorporate telemetry links, onboard cameras and external data modems, enabling mid-course corrections and, potentially, real-time control. This shifts the system from autonomous navigation to human-in-the-loop operation up to the terminal phase.

A moving passenger train is not an accidental target. Locomotives, freight wagons and passenger carriages are visually distinct, especially during low-altitude terminal guidance. Striking a mid-train passenger wagon strongly suggests situational awareness and conscious decision-making by the operator, not a navigation error.

Thisis what makes the incident operationally significant. Earlier Shahed/Geran use focused on static infrastructure. Engaging a moving civilian target implies either precise intelligence and timing or live visual confirmation. Both indicate a higher level of control and intent.

As a result, the character of the weapon changes. A loitering munition flying to coordinates can still be framed as a military strike tool. A loitering munition guided online onto a civilian target becomes a tool of terror. The decisive factor is not payload or range, but the preservation of human intent until the final seconds of flight.

This attack was not a technical malfunction or collateral damage caused by an autonomous system. It was the outcome of a weapon that has evolved into a remotely guided strike platform, with a human consciously making the final targeting decision.

That shift — more than the drone itself — defines the real escalation.

We deployed three acoustic detectors across the proving ground and launched the full system. The detection and tracking algorithms performed as expected throughout the tests.

Then we encountered something we hadn’t anticipated…

Heat.

While designing the system, we spent a lot of time thinking about operation in winter, rain, and other harsh field conditions. What we almost completely overlooked was prolonged operation under direct summer sunlight.

The aluminum enclosure absorbed much more heat than we had anticipated. As the internal temperature increased, we started seeing random subsystem restarts and intermittent packet loss. Once the devices cooled down, everything returned to normal.

It was a valuable lesson in humility.

You can spend months validating a system in the lab, but a single day in the field will often teach you something you never anticipated.

We returned from the proving ground not only with a successful demonstration behind us, but more importantly with a clear list of improvements to implement before the next round of testing. That’s exactly why initiatives like this matter.

If Poligon dla Innowatora becomes a monthly initiative, we’d like to make it an integral part of our development process. We’ll treat every proving ground session like the review at the end of a Scrum sprint: a few weeks of development, followed by validation in real field conditions. Every test will directly shape the next iteration of the product.

This is how robust defence technologies are built... through continuous testing, fast feedback, and constant improvement.

Looking forward to the next field test.
